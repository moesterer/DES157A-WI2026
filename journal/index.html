Best Practices for Modals / Overlays / Dialog Windows

February 11, 2026 • Original article

I read the article on modal windows, and the main takeaway I had is that it has to be used thoughtfully because it can be frustrating when it's not. I have mostly associated modals with pop-ups that interrupt my workflow so they can be frustrating just to take that single second to close, but the article helped me understand that for other people its purpose serves to focus their attention while still preserving the main context of the page. They're helpful because they can act as interfaces to help users from getting lost when it comes to page navigation. I've never even really stopped to read about them so this wasn't something that had occurred to me.

One of these navigation ideas was the idea of providing an escape hatch. In the past I have definitely come across websites that make it purposefully difficult to close a modal, with practically transparent X symbols that you need to find first in order to close them which created a lot of stress and distrust towards that website. So as the article emphasized, and as I very strongly resonate with, it's important to have a clear and close exit button as well as having the ability to just click outside the modal in order to escape from the window.

The concept that a modal should be user initiated was rather surprising, whenever I say a modal window it usually feels like the websites trying to shove something in my face, but changing the perspective to a designer's and remembering that some information is important to present to the user made me realize that these interruptions while annoying, should happen when something is truly important.

I still believe sites tend to overuse modals for marketing or for notifications and this leads people to automatically dismiss them. So I want to be more intentional when I choose to use this design pattern and not rely heavily on it as it is likely to be ignored.

Best practices for form design

February 9, 2026 • Original article

Whenever I get an email or notification about a form that needs to be filled out I automatically am filled with a sense of dread. No form is easy to fill out, every step seems annoying and tedious. This article tackles exactly that problem, and explains how thoughtful form design can significantly improve usability and completion rates. Just being on the other side of that, as a person filling out a form these kinds of designs are disappointingly rare.

One such tip was to minimize input fields. When a form only asks for the essential information, it reduces the cognitive load and makes the process feel a lot faster and much less overwhelming. There was also an emphasis on having clear labels, as avoiding placeholder only instructions is important because users must always be able to understand what information is actually required without having to guess.

I know that whenever I'm filling out a form there's always a time where I turn to a friend or a family member just to double check that I'm understanding the meaning and the information I'm putting in is correct. That's why as the article suggested, providing inline error messages and clear feedback helps prevent a lot of the frustration I and users have felt and guide them towards successful completion instead of leaving us confused about what we might be doing wrong.

Web form evaluation

It was hard to find an example of a form that embodies these best practices as most websites only have forms for delivery or sign ups which have lots of autofilled information. One of the strongest I found however is the DoorDash checkout address form. It looks simple at first but it does minimize all the required fields. It only asks for the essential contact and delivery information and all of the required entries are clearly marked, which reduces the decision fatigue. The labels are placed above the fields meaning the users don't get confused about what they're typing. It is a multi-step input process but it doesn't feel overwhelming.
