Game Design Research

March 2, 2026

I read the article on some principles of game design, and it helped me better understand how the dice game we built in class relates to these core principles. The article essentially lists a bunch of clear goals and objectives, with the dice game the primary goal was obvious: to reach the winning threshold before the other player. We set the winning condition and that single number created a whole structure and purpose. There were smaller goals too, like choosing whether to roll again or pass, trying to avoid snake eyes and roll safely which created a bit of tension which aligned with the article’s point that goals motivates players to keep moving forward.

The article also highlighted that engaging core mechanics and maintaining flow is important. The dice game the mechanics were fairly simple: click to roll, accumulate your points, and then decide whether to continue or pass. The structured interaction that updated through setUpTurn and throwDice were clear interface areas for game state, actions, and score. This separation that we implemented kept the experience readable and intuitive. So that when you play the game you aren't figuring out the controls but just focusing on the strategy of the game. This aligned with the article’s claim that mechanics should feel mindless and intuitive. The interface updated the dice images scores and such after each role which preserved flow and reinforced feedback.

Balancing difficulty and providing feedback is also a central topic. The dice game balanced randomness with risk. Rolling the Snake Eyes resets progress which as mentioned created tension come out while the score displays and when messages give the player immediate feedback and reward. If I were designing this game by myself without the class introduction, I imagine I would repeatedly test those small pieces before adding to their complexity which ties to the article’s emphasis on playtesting, this was also mentioned in class as a step that we should do when designing a game on our own.

Visual Thinking: Family and Cultural Identity

February 18, 2026

As I tackled this assignment late, I had no access to another person's photo or a photo I had no context for so I decided to do part two of this assignment twice and analyze to of the images I chose for this project.

The first image of children standing on a stage is of my Chinese School performance. At first glance it just looks like a group of children singing on stage but if you look closer we're all dressed in red and traditional clothing. Examining in even more detail you can see a banner that reads “East Bay Chinese School” above our heads with red lanterns and gold Chinese characters across the front of the stage which could clue a reader in to some sort of cultural significance.

What you might not know from just looking at this performance is that it represents a whole wave of second and third generation children trying to maintain their parents' languages and cultural traditions in the midst of the United States. It's a snapshot of immigrant communities creating spaces to pass down heritage, even in an environment so different from their ancestral homeland. As a kid I was put in Chinese School in order to attempt to pass on the Chinese language as part of my culture, it wasn't something I chose but rather something my parents chose for me. That reflects a lot of how I felt about my Chinese Heritage growing up. one way I would make the image more compelling is to zoom in on the details that might be hard to notice as they aren't in the foreground of the photo. For example, I would make sure to zoom in on the text of the banner and perhaps some of the decorations to clue the viewer in as to the cultural significance, rather than just kids standing on a stage.

The second image is of me as a baby being held by my grandmother at some sort of family gathering. You can't tell from looking at this image alone, but the entire side of my mother's family was there which meant all of my Chinese relatives, it's not just my grandmother and me. This photo represents a generational gathering, with significance in history and the reasoning behind where my culture comes from. Family is a huge emphasis, so it feels domestic and happy. This generational history and warm emotion is something I wanted to capture in relation to how I'm attached and how I interpret my own Chinese heritage. It all stems from family, people I grew up with and know, it's not just dna.

To enhance this photo I could perhaps tighten the frame around my grandmother and me to focus on our relationship, or perhaps include some sort of text overlay to explain who these people are in relation to me. Because another thing you wouldn't know just by looking at the photo is that my grandmother practically raised me while my parents were kick starting their careers. She moved in with us when I was very young and didn't leave until I was almost in high school.

Visual Thinking and Close Reading Through Google Arts & Culture

February 16, 2026 • Google Arts & Culture

One of the aspects of close reading of any text, whether written or visual, requires that the observer proceeds slowly and purposefully. It allows you to notice more details, make important connections and really absorb rather than just scroll. The website I found and chose to explore was the Google Arts & Culture page. Google itself is so vast but this site really invites slow browsing. Instead of just scrolling past images you can click on an artwork and it zooms you into really taking in details you otherwise might not have noticed.

The image really is the main focus of the site and it zooms in on brush strokes, things happening in the background, cracks of paint, and other things you'd normally miss in a museum. With its curated interactive exhibits, timelines, themes, and even interactive portions, this website, whose content is almost all images, really encourages connections between culture and time. The benefit of it being a web page is that you can spend a lot longer and get a lot closer to a single piece of artwork than you possibly could in person.

This website’s format allows you to really figure out what’s going on in the picture, and figure out what more you can find with high resolution images that are made to reward careful detailed observation. One drawback however is that the site could seem overwhelming to users who are not seeking that much information. Because arts and culture is such a broad topic and the web page tries to encapsulate all of it, it can be easier to skim rather than practice true close reading of the page, which defeats the purpose of having so much information readily accessible. Overall however, I think that this website does a good job of modeling how digital spaces can encourage visual literacy through observation and interpretation.

Best Practices for Modals / Overlays / Dialog Windows

February 11, 2026 • Original article

I read the article on modal windows, and the main takeaway I had is that it has to be used thoughtfully because it can be frustrating when it's not. I have mostly associated modals with pop-ups that interrupt my workflow so they can be frustrating just to take that single second to close, but the article helped me understand that for other people its purpose serves to focus their attention while still preserving the main context of the page. They're helpful because they can act as interfaces to help users from getting lost when it comes to page navigation. I've never even really stopped to read about them so this wasn't something that had occurred to me.

One of these navigation ideas was the idea of providing an escape hatch. In the past I have definitely come across websites that make it purposefully difficult to close a modal, with practically transparent X symbols that you need to find first in order to close them which created a lot of stress and distrust towards that website. So as the article emphasized, and as I very strongly resonate with, it's important to have a clear and close exit button as well as having the ability to just click outside the modal in order to escape from the window.

The concept that a modal should be user initiated was rather surprising, whenever I say a modal window it usually feels like the websites trying to shove something in my face, but changing the perspective to a designer's and remembering that some information is important to present to the user made me realize that these interruptions while annoying, should happen when something is truly important.

I still believe sites tend to overuse modals for marketing or for notifications and this leads people to automatically dismiss them. So I want to be more intentional when I choose to use this design pattern and not rely heavily on it as it is likely to be ignored.

Best practices for form design

February 9, 2026 • Original article

Whenever I get an email or notification about a form that needs to be filled out I automatically am filled with a sense of dread. No form is easy to fill out, every step seems annoying and tedious. This article tackles exactly that problem, and explains how thoughtful form design can significantly improve usability and completion rates. Just being on the other side of that, as a person filling out a form these kinds of designs are disappointingly rare.

One such tip was to minimize input fields. When a form only asks for the essential information, it reduces the cognitive load and makes the process feel a lot faster and much less overwhelming. There was also an emphasis on having clear labels, as avoiding placeholder only instructions is important because users must always be able to understand what information is actually required without having to guess.

I know that whenever I'm filling out a form there's always a time where I turn to a friend or a family member just to double check that I'm understanding the meaning and the information I'm putting in is correct. That's why as the article suggested, providing inline error messages and clear feedback helps prevent a lot of the frustration I and users have felt and guide them towards successful completion instead of leaving us confused about what we might be doing wrong.

Web form evaluation

It was hard to find an example of a form that embodies these best practices as most websites only have forms for delivery or sign ups which have lots of autofilled information. One of the strongest I found however is the DoorDash checkout address form. It looks simple at first but it does minimize all the required fields. It only asks for the essential contact and delivery information and all of the required entries are clearly marked, which reduces the decision fatigue. The labels are placed above the fields meaning the users don't get confused about what they're typing. It is a multi-step input process but it doesn't feel overwhelming.
